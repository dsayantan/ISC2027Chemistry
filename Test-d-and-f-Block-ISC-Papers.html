Q1General properties of the transition elements1 mark
Zinc, cadmium and mercury are d-block elements but are not regarded as transition elements because:
ExplanationA transition element must have a partly filled d subshell either as the atom or in a common oxidation state. All three are d10 as the atom and d10 as M2+, so neither condition is met.Source · ISC 2018 Q1(d)(i)(2) · ISC 2024 Q3(i) · Spec 2023 Q11 · Spec 2024 Q5(ii)
Q2General properties of the transition elements1 mark
The density of the 3d elements increases from titanium (Z = 22) to copper (Z = 29) because:
ExplanationDensity is mass divided by volume. Across the series the nuclear charge and the shielding by the added 3d electrons very nearly cancel, so the atomic volume barely changes while the mass keeps climbing — and the density climbs with it.Source · ISC 2018 Q1(d)(i)(1) · ISC 2020 Q15(ii)
Q3General properties of the transition elements1 mark
Transition elements have high melting points and high enthalpies of atomisation because:
ExplanationBoth the ns and the unpaired (n−1)d electrons are available for metallic bonding, so the bond is much stronger than in an s-block metal. The enthalpy of atomisation therefore peaks near the middle of each series, where the unpaired electrons are most numerous.Source · Spec 2018 Q1(iii) · ISC 2025-Improvement Q20(i)(a)
Q4General properties of the transition elements1 mark
Which element of the 3d series has the lowest enthalpy of atomisation, and why?
ExplanationZinc is 3d104s2. With every d electron paired, only the 4s pair is left to bond, so the metallic bond is the weakest in the series — which is also why zinc, cadmium and mercury melt so low.Source · Spec 2018 Q1(iii)
Q5General properties of the transition elements1 mark
The melting and boiling points of Zn, Cd and Hg are low because:
ExplanationThe same cause as the low enthalpy of atomisation. A filled d10 subshell contributes nothing to the metallic bond, so only the two ns electrons hold the lattice together. Mercury is liquid at room temperature for this reason.Source · Spec 2026 Q8(ii) · Spec 2027 Q7(ii)
Q6General properties of the transition elements1 mark
Transition elements show variable oxidation states because:
ExplanationThe small energy gap means the d electrons can be removed one at a time after the ns pair has gone, giving states that differ by one — Mn runs from +2 to +7. In the p block the energy gap is large and oxidation states usually differ by two.Source · Spec 2018 Q13(a)(i) · Spec 2023 Q3(ii)
Q7General properties of the transition elements1 mark
Transition metals form a large number of complex compounds because of:
ExplanationA small, highly charged cation attracts ligands strongly, and the empty d orbitals are of the right energy to accept the donated lone pairs. The variable oxidation states mean it can do so in more than one form.Source · ISC 2024 Q3(ii) · Spec 2023 Q3(i)
Q8General properties of the transition elements1 mark
Transition metals and their compounds act as catalysts chiefly because:
ExplanationTwo mechanisms. In homogeneous catalysis the metal shuttles between oxidation states, as Fe2+/Fe3+ does between iodide and persulphate. In heterogeneous catalysis the reactants are adsorbed on the surface through the vacant d orbitals, which weakens their bonds.Source · ISC 2019 Q13(a)(iii) · ISC 2023 Q11(ii) · Spec 2018 Q13(a)(iii)
Q9General properties of the transition elements1 mark
Transition metals form alloys with one another very readily because:
ExplanationThese are substitutional alloys: one atom simply takes another's place in the lattice, which requires the two radii to be within about 15% of one another. Across the d block they are. Contrast an interstitial compound, where a much smaller atom fills a hole.Source · ISC 2019 Q13(a)(i) · Spec 2027 Q15(ii)
Q10General properties of the transition elements1 mark
Which pair of 3d elements has an exceptional electronic configuration?
ExplanationChromium is 3d54s1 and copper 3d104s1, not the expected 3d44s2 and 3d94s2. The half-filled and fully-filled d subshells are stabilised by exchange energy, which outweighs the cost of promoting a 4s electron.Source · Spec 2023 Q1(iii)
Q11General properties of the transition elements1 mark
The third ionisation energy of manganese (Z = 25) is unexpectedly high because:
ExplanationThe third electron has to come out of a half-filled d5 set, which carries maximum exchange energy. Breaking it is costly, so IE3 is high — and this is also why Mn2+ is so stable against oxidation to +3.Source · Spec 2024 Q3(ii)
Q12General properties of the transition elements1 mark
What is the basic difference between the electronic configurations of the transition and the inner transition elements?
ExplanationThe general configurations are (n−1)d1–10ns1–2 for the d block and (n−2)f1–14(n−1)d0–1ns2 for the f block. The f electrons are two shells in, which is why they take so little part in bonding.Source · Spec 2022 Semester 2 Q12(i)
Q13Colour and magnetic behaviour1 mark
Transition metal ions are usually coloured because of:
ExplanationA ligand field splits the five d orbitals into two sets a small energy apart. That gap matches visible light, so an electron absorbs a photon and is promoted, and the ion appears in the colour complementary to the light absorbed.Source · ISC 2018 Q13(a)(ii) · Spec 2027 Q1(iii)
Q14Colour and magnetic behaviour1 mark
Transition metal ions are colourless when their d subshell is:
ExplanationA d–d transition needs an electron to promote and a vacancy to promote it into. A d0 ion such as Sc3+ has no electron; a d10 ion such as Zn2+ has no vacancy. Either way nothing in the visible region is absorbed, and such ions are also diamagnetic.Source · ISC 2025 Q1(i)(iv)
Q15Colour and magnetic behaviour1 mark
Salts of Cu+ are colourless while salts of Cu2+ are coloured because:
ExplanationCopper is [Ar]3d104s1. Losing the 4s electron gives Cu+, d10 and full; losing one more gives Cu2+, d9 with one vacancy and one unpaired electron. The same difference makes Zn2+ (d10) white and Ni2+ (d8) green.Source · ISC 2019 Q13(a)(ii) · ISC 2024 Q5(ii) · Spec 2022 Semester 2 Q12(ii) · Spec 2024 Q5(i)
Q16Colour and magnetic behaviour1 mark
Cu2+ is paramagnetic but Cu+ is diamagnetic because:
ExplanationParamagnetism comes from unpaired spins and nothing else. μ = √n(n+2) BM gives 1·73 BM for the one unpaired electron of Cu2+ and exactly zero for Cu+.Source · ISC 2019 Q1(d)(i)(2) · ISC 2023 Q3(i) · Spec 2022 Semester 2 Q1(ii)
Q17Colour and magnetic behaviour1 mark
Transition metals and their compounds generally show paramagnetic behaviour and are attracted into a magnetic field because:
ExplanationParamagnetism is a property of unpaired electron spins. Because a transition metal can lose electrons to reach many different dn configurations, most of its ions have unpaired electrons and most of its compounds are therefore paramagnetic.Source · ISC 2020 Q15(i) · Spec 2025 Q20(i)(b)
Q18Colour and magnetic behaviour1 mark
Of Co2+ (Z = 27) and Zn2+ (Z = 30), which is attracted into a magnetic field?
ExplanationCobalt is [Ar]3d74s2, so Co2+ is 3d7 — three unpaired electrons, μ = 3·87 BM, paramagnetic. Zinc is 3d104s2, so Zn2+ is 3d10, all paired and diamagnetic.Source · ISC 2026 Q13(ii) · Spec 2027 Q7(i)
Q19Colour and magnetic behaviour1 mark
Which divalent ion of the 3d series has the greatest paramagnetic character with a weak field ligand?
ExplanationIn a weak field the electrons spread out over all five d orbitals before pairing. Mn2+ is d5, so all five are unpaired — the largest number any ion can have, giving μ = √35 = 5·92 BM.Source · Spec 2026 Q8(i)
Q20Colour and magnetic behaviour1 mark
Cu2+ and Fe3+ generally form coloured compounds because:
ExplanationCu2+ is d9 and Fe3+ is d5. Each has electrons in the lower set and vacancies in the upper set, which is exactly what a d–d transition needs.Source · ISC 2025-Improvement Q20(i)(c)
Q21Colour and magnetic behaviour1 mark
Mn2+ is more stable than Fe2+ towards oxidation to the +3 state because:
ExplanationMn2+ is d5 and oxidising it would destroy the half-filled set, so it resists. Fe2+ is d6, and losing one electron reaches d5 — so iron(II) is readily oxidised while manganese(II) is not.Source · ISC 2018 Q13(a)(i) · ISC 2023 Q3(ii) · ISC 2024 Q11(i) · Spec 2025 Q20(i)(c)
Q22Lanthanoids and actinoids1 mark
Zirconium (Z = 40) and hafnium (Z = 72) have almost identical radii and are very difficult to separate because of:
ExplanationThe fourteen lanthanoids lie between them. The steady shrinkage across that series exactly offsets the increase in size expected on adding a shell, so Zr and Hf — and Nb/Ta, Mo/W — are nearly the same size and behave almost identically.Source · ISC 2018 Q13(a)(iii) · ISC 2024 Q5(i) · ISC 2025 Q1 assertion · ISC 2025-Improvement Q20(i)(b) · Spec 2018 Q13(a)(ii)
Q23Lanthanoids and actinoids1 mark
The size of the trivalent cations across the lanthanoid series:
ExplanationThis is lanthanoid contraction. Each added 4f electron screens the nucleus poorly, so the effective nuclear charge felt by the outer electrons rises steadily and the ion shrinks. A statement that lanthanoid size increases with atomic number — as one specimen assertion puts it — is false.Source · Spec 2024 Q3(i) · Spec 2025 Q1(vii) assertion
Q24Lanthanoids and actinoids1 mark
Which trivalent ion is the largest in the lanthanoid series, from La3+ (Z = 57) to Lu3+ (Z = 71)?
ExplanationBecause of lanthanoid contraction the radii fall steadily across the series, so the first member is the largest and the last the smallest. Lanthanum is the first, lutetium the last.Source · ISC 2019 Q1(d)(i)(1)
Q25Lanthanoids and actinoids1 mark
La(OH)3 is more basic than Lu(OH)3 because:
ExplanationLanthanoid contraction makes Lu3+ the smallest and most polarising of the series, so Lu—OH is the most covalent and the least basic. Basic strength therefore falls from La to Lu. Note the reason offered in one improvement-paper assertion — that the cations grow across the series — is false.Source · ISC 2023 Q11(i) · ISC 2025-Improvement Q1(vii) assertion
Q26Lanthanoids and actinoids1 mark
Why are La3+ (Z = 57) and Lu3+ (Z = 71) colourless in solution?
ExplanationThe lanthanoids are coloured by f–f transitions, which need a partly filled 4f subshell. These two are the extremes: lanthanum has no 4f electron to promote and lutetium has no vacancy to promote one into. The parallel with d0 and d10 in the d block is exact.Source · ISC 2024 Q11(ii)
Q27Potassium permanganate and dichromate1 mark
Complete: 2KMnO4 + 8H2SO4 + 10FeSO4 → ?
ExplanationMnO4− + 8H+ + 5e− → Mn2+ + 4H2O, and Fe2+ → Fe3+ + e−. One permanganate oxidises five iron(II), so the ratio is 1 : 5 — the same stoichiometry as the Mohr salt titration. Count the water: 16 H+ give 8 H2O.Source · ISC 2018 Q13(b)(iii) · Spec 2023 · Spec 2025 Q20(ii)(a) · ISC 2026 Q13(i)(b)
Q28Potassium permanganate and dichromate1 mark
Complete: 2KMnO4 + 10KI + 8H2SO4 → ?
ExplanationIodide is oxidised to iodine, I− → ½I2 + e−, so again five iodides per permanganate. Check the potassium: 2 from KMnO4 and 10 from KI makes 12, which is six K2SO4.Source · ISC 2018 Q13(b)(i) · Spec 2026 Q13(i)(b)
Q29Potassium permanganate and dichromate1 mark
Complete: 2KMnO4 + 3H2SO4 + 5H2S → ?
ExplanationSulphide is oxidised only as far as free sulphur, S2− → S + 2e−, so five H2S supply the ten electrons two permanganates need. The milkiness of the solution is that sulphur.Source · ISC 2025-Improvement Q20(ii)(b) · Spec 2027 Q15(i) · ISC 2018 Q13(b)(ii)
Q30Potassium permanganate and dichromate1 mark
The purple colour of KMnO4 disappears in an acidified solution of oxalic acid because:
Explanation2MnO4− + 16H+ + 5C2O42− → 2Mn2+ + 10CO2 + 8H2O. The deep purple MnO4− becomes the very pale pink Mn2+, which is why this titration needs no indicator.Source · ISC 2019 Q13(b)(i) · Spec 2018 Q13(b)(iii) · Spec 2025 Q20(i)(a)
Q31Potassium permanganate and dichromate1 mark
SO2 decolourises the pink colour of an acidified KMnO4 solution because:
Explanation2MnO4− + 5SO2 + 2H2O → 2Mn2+ + 5SO42− + 4H+. Sulphur goes from +4 to +6; the decolourisation is a redox change, not bleaching.Source · Spec 2022 Semester 1, assertion
Q32Potassium permanganate and dichromate1 mark
Complete: K2Cr2O7 + 7H2SO4 + 6KI → ?
ExplanationCr2O72− + 14H+ + 6e− → 2Cr3+ + 7H2O, so six electrons are needed and six iodides supply them. The orange solution turns green as Cr(VI) becomes Cr(III).Source · ISC 2019 Q13(b)(ii) · Spec 2023 · Spec 2025 Q20(ii)(b) · ISC 2026 Q13(i)(a)
Q33Potassium permanganate and dichromate1 mark
Complete: K2Cr2O7 + 7H2SO4 + 6FeSO4 → ?
ExplanationSix electrons again, this time from six Fe2+. Only one K2SO4 here, because the only potassium present is the two atoms of the dichromate — unlike the KI reaction, where the iodide brings six more.Source · ISC 2019 Q13(b)(iii) · Spec 2018 Q13(b)(i) · Spec 2026 Q13(i)(a)
Q34Potassium permanganate and dichromate1 mark
Complete: K2Cr2O7 + H2SO4 + 3Na2SO3 → ?
ExplanationSulphite is oxidised to sulphate, SO32− → SO42−, giving two electrons each; three of them supply the six the dichromate needs. The equation balances with 4H2SO4 on the left.Source · ISC 2025-Improvement Q20(ii)(a)
Q35Potassium permanganate and dichromate1 mark
Acidified K2Cr2O7 solution turns green when Na2SO3 is added. The green colour is due to the formation of:
ExplanationThe sulphite reduces Cr(VI) to Cr(III), and chromium(III) sulphate is green. Chromate is yellow, and the oxide would be a precipitate, not a colour in solution.Source · Spec 2024 Q1(iii)
Q36Potassium permanganate and dichromate1 mark
K2Cr2O7 acts as a powerful oxidising agent in acidic medium because:
ExplanationCr2O72− + 14H+ + 6e− → 2Cr3+ + 7H2O. The 14 H+ are why the medium must be acidic — in alkali the ion becomes chromate instead and the oxidising power is lost.Source · ISC 2020 Q15(iii) · Spec 2027 Q15(iii)
Q37Potassium permanganate and dichromate1 mark
How does the colour of a K2Cr2O7 solution depend on pH?
ExplanationCr2O72− + 2OH− → 2CrO42− + H2O, and the reverse on acidifying. The two ions are in equilibrium, so the colour is a direct reading of the pH.Source · Spec 2026 Q13(ii)
