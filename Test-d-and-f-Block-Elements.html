Q1Why they are transition elements1 mark
A d-block element is classed as a transition element because it has:
ExplanationThe definition turns on the d subshell being partly filled. It is enough for this to be true of the neutral atom or of a common oxidation state — which is why scandium counts even though Sc3+ is d0.Source · Note 1
Q2Why they are transition elements1 mark
Zinc, cadmium and mercury sit in the d block but are not regarded as transition elements because:
ExplanationAll three are d10 as the atom and d10 as M2+. Nothing is left partly filled in either, so the definition in item 1 is never satisfied.Source · Note 2
Q3Why they are transition elements1 mark
Sc3+ has a d0 configuration, yet scandium is counted as a transition metal. Why?
ExplanationThe definition allows either the atom or a stable ion to carry the partly filled d subshell. Scandium qualifies on the atom, which is 3d14s2. Its only common ion is d0, so it is colourless and diamagnetic.Source · Note 3
Q4Why they are transition elements1 mark
The atomic radii of the first transition series fall at first and then change very little. The reason is:
ExplanationElectrons are being added to an inner 3d subshell while the outermost shell stays 4s. The shielding those 3d electrons provide roughly cancels the extra nuclear charge, so after the first few elements the radius is nearly constant.Source · Note 4
Q5Why they are transition elements1 mark
Zirconium and hafnium have almost identical atomic radii because of:
ExplanationThe fourteen lanthanoids come between them. The steady contraction across that series cancels the increase in size expected on going from the second to the third transition series, so Zr and Hf — and Nb/Ta, Mo/W — are nearly the same size and very hard to separate.Source · Note 5
Q6Why they are transition elements1 mark
Ionisation enthalpies across the first transition series rise irregularly rather than smoothly because:
ExplanationHalf-filled and fully-filled d configurations are extra stable because of exchange energy, so the energy needed to reach them — or to break them — varies from element to element. The note sheet writes ‘exchange of energy between d and f orbitals’; there are no f electrons in the first series, so read that as exchange energy within the d subshell.Source · Note 6
Q7Why they are transition elements1 mark
Transition metals have strong metallic bonding, high enthalpies of atomisation and high melting points because of:
ExplanationThe more unpaired d electrons available for bonding, the stronger the metallic bond. That is why enthalpy of atomisation peaks near the middle of each series, where the number of unpaired electrons is greatest.Source · Note 7
Q8Why they are transition elements1 mark
Zinc, cadmium and mercury are soft and melt at low temperatures because:
ExplanationWith a full d10 subshell there are no unpaired electrons to contribute to metallic bonding. Their enthalpies of atomisation are the lowest in the series — mercury is liquid at room temperature.Source · Note 8
Q9Why they are transition elements1 mark
Transition elements show a large number of oxidation states because:
ExplanationThe energy gap between (n−1)d and ns is small, so after the ns electrons are lost the d electrons can be lost one at a time. That gives a run of oxidation states differing by one — unlike the p block, where they usually differ by two.Source · Note 9
Q10Oxidation states and electrode potentials1 mark
Which element of the first transition series shows the greatest number of oxidation states, and why?
ExplanationManganese is 3d54s2 — five unpaired d electrons plus two 4s. Every one can be used, giving oxidation states from +2 right through to +7, the maximum for the series.Source · Note 10
Q11Oxidation states and electrode potentials1 mark
Which element of the first transition series does not show variable oxidation states, and why?
ExplanationScandium is 3d14s2. Losing all three leaves [Ar], which is so stable that +3 is effectively its only oxidation state.Source · Note 11
Q12Oxidation states and electrode potentials1 mark
Cr3+ is more stable than Cr2+ because:
ExplanationIn an octahedral field the d3 ion puts one electron in each of the three t2g orbitals — a half-filled set with maximum exchange energy. Cr2+ is therefore a good reducing agent, losing an electron to reach it.Source · Note 12
Q13Oxidation states and electrode potentials1 mark
In aqueous solution Cu2+ is more stable than Cu+ because:
ExplanationCu+ is d10 and looks the more stable configuration — and in the gas phase it is. In water the smaller, doubly charged Cu2+ is hydrated far more strongly, and that releases more than enough energy to remove the second electron. Cu+ therefore disproportionates in solution.Source · Note 13
Q14Oxidation states and electrode potentials1 mark
Which first-series transition metal shows a +1 oxidation state, and why?
ExplanationLosing the single 4s electron leaves copper as 3d10. The state is well known in the gaseous phase and in insoluble or complexed compounds such as CuCl and [Cu(NH3)2]+, though it disproportionates in water (item 13).Source · Note 14
Q15Oxidation states and electrode potentials1 mark
The highest oxidation states of the transition metals are met with in their oxides and fluorides because:
ExplanationA metal in a high oxidation state is strongly oxidising; only the most electronegative, least easily oxidised partners can survive beside it. Hence OsO4, Mn2O7 and CrF6.Source · Note 15
Q16Oxidation states and electrode potentials1 mark
Why are the very highest oxidation states more common in the oxides than in the fluorides of transition metals?
ExplanationFluorine forms only a single bond, so a high oxidation state needs a large number of fluorine atoms round the metal and the structure becomes crowded. Oxygen can donate a lone pair into an empty metal d orbital as well, forming a dπ–pπ bond, so one oxygen does the work of two fluorines. Note fluorine is in fact the more electronegative; the multiple bonding, not electronegativity, is the reason.Source · Note 16
Q17Oxidation states and electrode potentials1 mark
Across the first transition series from left to right the +2 oxidation state becomes increasingly stable because:
ExplanationEarly in the series the d electrons are readily used, so high oxidation states are common. As the subshell fills they become more tightly held and less available, until near the end only the two 4s electrons are easily lost and +2 dominates.Source · Note 17
Q18Oxidation states and electrode potentials1 mark
In which of these oxoanions is the oxidation number of the metal not equal to its group number?
ExplanationManganese is in group 7. In the manganate ion MnO42− it is +6, one short. In permanganate it is +7, in dichromate chromium is +6 (group 6) and in the metavanadate ion vanadium is +5 (group 5) — all three match their group.Source · Note 18
Q19Oxidation states and electrode potentials1 mark
Across the first series E°(M2+/M) becomes less negative, but irregularly. The irregularity is due to:
ExplanationThe electrode potential is set by enthalpy of atomisation, IE1 + IE2, and hydration enthalpy together. The ionisation term is the one that varies irregularly — for the same exchange-energy reasons as item 6 — and it carries the irregularity into E°.Source · Note 19
Q20Oxidation states and electrode potentials1 mark
E°(Cu2+/Cu) is positive, +0·34 V, unlike the rest of the first series. Why?
ExplanationFor every other metal in the series the energy released on hydrating M2+ outweighs what it costs to atomise and ionise the metal, so E° is negative and the metal displaces hydrogen. For copper the sum ΔsubH + IE1 + IE2 is unusually high and is not repaid, so copper does not liberate hydrogen from acids.Source · Note 20
Q21Oxidation states and electrode potentials1 mark
E°(Mn3+/Mn2+) is strongly positive, so Mn3+ is a powerful oxidising agent. Why?
ExplanationMn3+ is d4. Gaining one electron takes it to d5, half filled and extra stable, so the reduction is very favourable.Source · Note 21
Q22Oxidation states and electrode potentials1 mark
E°(Cr3+/Cr2+) is negative, so Cr2+ is a reducing agent. Why?
ExplanationThis is the mirror image of item 21. There the ion was reduced to reach a stable configuration; here the stable configuration is the oxidised form, so Cr2+ gives an electron away to reach it.Source · Note 22
Q23Colour, magnetism and characteristic properties1 mark
Most transition metal compounds are coloured, both as solids and in solution, because of:
ExplanationA ligand field splits the five d orbitals into two sets a small energy apart. That gap happens to match visible light, so an electron can absorb a photon and jump, and the compound shows the colour complementary to the light absorbed.Source · Note 23
Q24Colour, magnetism and characteristic properties1 mark
Sc3+ and Ti4+ are colourless because:
ExplanationA d–d transition needs an electron in the lower set and a vacancy in the upper set. These two ions have no d electrons at all, so nothing can be promoted and nothing in the visible region is absorbed.Source · Note 24
Q25Colour, magnetism and characteristic properties1 mark
Zn2+ and Cu+ are colourless because:
ExplanationThe opposite case to item 24, with the same consequence. The d subshell is completely full, so although there are plenty of d electrons there is nowhere for one to go. Both d0 and d10 ions are colourless.Source · Note 25
Q26Colour, magnetism and characteristic properties1 mark
Transition metals and their ions are usually paramagnetic because:
ExplanationParamagnetism comes from unpaired electron spins. The magnetic moment is √n(n+2) BM, with n the number of unpaired electrons — so a d0 or d10 ion is diamagnetic.Source · Note 26
Q27Colour, magnetism and characteristic properties1 mark
Transition metals form a very large number of complexes. Which set of reasons is correct?
ExplanationAll three matter. A small, highly charged ion polarises and attracts ligands strongly; the empty d orbitals of the right energy accept the donated lone pairs; and because the metal has several oxidation states it can do this in more than one form.Source · Note 27
Q28Colour, magnetism and characteristic properties1 mark
An interstitial compound is one in which:
ExplanationThey are not stoichiometric compounds in the ordinary sense: the formulae are often non-integral, such as TiC or Fe3H, because the small atoms simply fill holes that happen to be available.Source · Note 28
Q29Colour, magnetism and characteristic properties1 mark
Transition metals form interstitial compounds readily because:
ExplanationThe close-packed metal lattice leaves octahedral and tetrahedral holes. Atoms as small as hydrogen, carbon or nitrogen fit into them without disturbing the structure.Source · Note 29
Q30Colour, magnetism and characteristic properties1 mark
Why are interstitial compounds more useful than the pure metals?
ExplanationThe trapped atoms key the layers together so they cannot slide, which is what makes the material hard and raises the melting point. Steel is the everyday example — carbon in an iron lattice.Source · Note 30
Q31Colour, magnetism and characteristic properties1 mark
Transition metals and their compounds are widely used as catalysts because of:
ExplanationTwo mechanisms, and the reasons cover both. In heterogeneous catalysis the reactants are adsorbed on the surface using the vacant d orbitals, which weakens their bonds; in homogeneous catalysis the metal shuttles between oxidation states, as iron does in the reaction of iodide with persulphate.Source · Note 31
Q32Colour, magnetism and characteristic properties1 mark
Transition metals form alloys with one another very readily because:
ExplanationAlloys of this kind are substitutional: one metal atom simply takes the place of another. That needs the two radii to be close, which across the d block they are. Contrast the interstitial compounds of items 28–30, where the added atom is much smaller and goes into a hole.Source · Note 32
Q33Colour, magnetism and characteristic properties1 mark
Oxides of transition metals in high oxidation states are acidic while those in low oxidation states are basic. Why?
ExplanationCompare the chromium oxides: CrO is basic, Cr2O3 amphoteric and CrO3 acidic. The rising charge concentrates on a shrinking ion, pulls the oxide electron cloud towards itself and makes the bond covalent — and covalent oxides are acidic.Source · Note 33
Q34The f-block: lanthanoids and actinoids1 mark
Actinoids show a greater number of oxidation states than the lanthanoids because:
ExplanationIn the lanthanoids the 4f electrons are buried and take almost no part in bonding, so +3 dominates. In the actinoids 5f, 6d and 7s lie close together, so oxidation states from +3 up to +7 are known — uranium alone shows +3, +4, +5 and +6.Source · Note 34
Q35The f-block: lanthanoids and actinoids1 mark
The chemistry of the actinoids is difficult to study because:
ExplanationBeyond uranium the elements are synthetic and are made in minute quantities; the short half-lives mean the sample is decaying while it is being worked on, and the radioactivity itself makes handling hazardous.Source · Note 35
Q36The f-block: lanthanoids and actinoids1 mark
Ce(IV) is a good oxidising agent in aqueous solution because:
Explanation+3 is the normal oxidation state across the lanthanoids, so a lanthanoid forced into +4 reverts to it readily. The large positive potential is what makes ceric ammonium sulphate a standard oxidising agent in volumetric analysis.Source · Note 36
Q37The f-block: lanthanoids and actinoids1 mark
Actinoid contraction is greater, element for element, than lanthanoid contraction because:
ExplanationBoth contractions have the same cause — f electrons are poor at screening, so the effective nuclear charge felt by the outer electrons climbs steadily. The 5f orbitals extend further out and screen even worse than the 4f, so each step shrinks the atom more.Source · Note 37
