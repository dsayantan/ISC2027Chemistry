Oxidation–reduction titration — KMnO₄ against Mohr's salt

Heritage 2024–25 First Term, Question 1

The question

You are provided with two solutions as follows:
  • C-10 is a solution containing 1·08 g of potassium manganate(VII), KMnO4, per litre.
  • C-11 is a solution prepared by dissolving 14·7 g of hydrated ammonium iron(II) sulphate crystals, (NH4)2SO4·FeSO4·6H2O, per litre.
PROCEDURE: Rinse and fill the burette with C-10. Pipette 20 mL of C-11 into a clean conical flask and add 20 mL of C-12 (dilute sulphuric acid). Titrate until one drop gives a light permanent pink. Obtain two concordant readings.
Calculate: (i) the molarity of C-10, (ii) the molarity of C-11, (iii) the strength of C-11, (iv) the percentage purity.

What I would do

  1. Rinse the burette with C-10 and the pipette with C-11, so that no water is left to dilute either solution.
  2. Pipette 20 mL of C-11 into a clean conical flask and add 20 mL of dilute sulphuric acid. Add no indicator.
  3. Run C-10 in slowly, swirling, until one drop gives a pink that survives shaking.
  4. Read the burette at the upper meniscus — permanganate is too dark to see through — and repeat for two concordant readings.

What I should observe

The solution stays pale green while iron(II) remains; the moment it is all oxidised, one further drop colours the flask a permanent light pink.

The reaction, in full

Ionic equation
2MnO4− + 10Fe2+ + 16H+ → 2Mn2+ + 10Fe3+ + 8H2O
This is the one the calculation uses: 2 : 10, i.e. 1 : 5.
Oxidation half

× 5 — iron(II) loses one electron

The calculation

M1 = strength ÷ 158 = 1·08 ÷ 158 = 0·0068 M(i) molarity of C-10

Strength = M2 × 392 = 0·036570 × 392 = 14·34 g L−1(iii) strength
% purity = (14·34 ÷ 14·7) × 100 = 97·52 %(iv) purity

What I conclude

Readings 2 and 3 agree, so the titre is 21·4 mL. The sample is about 97½% pure, which is what an ordinary bottle of Mohr's salt gives: it effloresces and its iron(II) is slowly air-oxidised.

From the First Term paper  ·  Paper Q2

Identification of two organic compounds

Heritage 2024–25 First Term, Question 2

The question

You are provided with two organic compounds C-13 and C-14. Perform the experiments given below on each. Record the changes taking place at every step, note the smell of any substance formed, the colour of the solution and of any precipitate, and state the identity of each compound.
(a) C-13: (i) iodine crystals then NaOH until the colour goes, warm and cool; (ii) sodium nitroprusside then dilute NaOH; (iii) saturated NaHSO3, shake and warm.
(b) C-14: (i) Fehling's solution, warm; (ii) Tollens' reagent, warm; (iii) resorcinol then conc. H2SO4 down the side.

What I would do

  1. Work on 1–2 mL portions in clean tubes, one test at a time.
  2. For C-13: add iodine, then NaOH drop by drop only until the brown colour just goes; warm gently and cool.
  3. For C-14: use a water bath, not a direct flame — Tollens' reagent must not be boiled.
  4. Pour the concentrated acid down the side of the tube for the resorcinol test and do not shake it.

What I should observe

C-13 gives a yellow precipitate with a characteristic antiseptic smell, a violet colour with nitroprusside and a white crystalline addition compound with bisulphite. C-14 reduces both Fehling's and Tollens', and gives a red ring with resorcinol.

The white crystalline addition compound; reversed by dilute acid or alkali, which is how the ketone is recovered.

A coordination complex whose composition is not required at this level; the test responds to the enolisable C—H next to the carbonyl.

A condensation of the aldehyde with the phenol; the product is a dye of unstated structure.

Taken together the first three make C-13 a methyl ketone, and the simplest is acetone (propanone), CH3COCH3. The last three make C-14 an aldehyde, and the resorcinol ring makes it formaldehyde (methanal), HCHO. Note that no single test would do: iodoform alone also fits ethanol, and Fehling's alone also fits glucose.

Qualitative analysis of a salt

Heritage 2024–25 First Term, Question 3

The question

Analyse qualitatively the substance C-15, which contains one anion and one cation. Identify these ions.
(a) While testing for the anion you must mention (i) how the gases were identified and (ii) the confirmatory test.
(b) While testing for the cation you must mention (i) how the original solution was prepared, (ii) the formal group analysis with the group reagents and (iii) the confirmatory test.
Answered here for cobalt(II) nitrate, since the paper sets an unknown.

What I would do

  1. Note the colour and texture first: pale red, crystalline.
  2. Anion, dry: heat a little of the salt with conc. H2SO4 and copper turnings.
  3. Anion, wet: make the sodium carbonate extract, acidify it, add fresh FeSO4, then pour conc. H2SO4 down the side.
  4. Cation: dissolve a little salt in distilled water (the original solution) and work down the groups in order, filtering after each.

What I should observe

The dry test gives a reddish-brown gas. The brown ring forms at the junction of the two liquids and is destroyed if the tube is shaken. Groups I to III give nothing; ammoniacal H2S gives a black precipitate.

… and the ring itself

Nitrosoferrous sulphate — the brown ring. Its iron is still +2 and the NO is a neutral ligand.

The anion is NO3− and the cation is Co2+, so the salt is Co(NO3)2. The three rows recording no precipitate are not padding — they are what proves the cation is in Group IV rather than above it, and the marks for ‘formal group analysis’ are for exactly those rows.

Volumetric analysis  ·  Experiment 2A

Water of crystallisation in oxalic acid

School notes, cl 12 EXPERIMENT NO. — new

The question

You are provided with two solutions as follows:
  • C-10 contains 2·89 g of KMnO4 per litre.
  • C-11 contains 6·3 g per litre of hydrated oxalic acid crystals, H2C2O4·xH2O.
Titrate and calculate (i) the molarity of C-10, (ii) the molarity of C-11, (iii) the molecular weight of the hydrated crystals and (iv) the value of x.

What I would do

  1. Fill the burette with C-10 and pipette 20 mL of C-11 into a conical flask with 20 mL of dilute sulphuric acid.
  2. Warm the flask to about 65 °C before titrating. This is the one difference from the Mohr salt titration and it is not optional — cold, the reaction is far too slow and the first drops of permanganate sit unreacted.
  3. Titrate the warm solution slowly to the first permanent pink. Do not let it boil.
  4. Take two concordant readings.

What I should observe

The first few drops are slow to decolourise even when warm; once Mn2+ has formed the reaction speeds up markedly, because Mn2+ autocatalyses it. At the end point one drop gives a permanent light pink.

Sl. no.Initial (mL)Final (mL)Volume used (mL)
10·022·022·0
20·021·921·9
30·021·921·9

The equations

The reaction, in full
2KMnO4 + 3H2SO4 + 5H2C2O4 → K2SO4 + 2MnSO4 + 10CO2↑ + 8H2O
Ionic equation
2MnO4− + 16H+ + 5C2O42− → 2Mn2+ + 10CO2↑ + 8H2O
2 : 5 this time, not 1 : 5 — one oxalate ion gives up two electrons, so five of them reduce two permanganate ions. The school notes print 5CO2 here; it must be 10CO2, as their own molecular equation on the same page has.
Oxidation half
C2O42− → 2CO2↑ + 2e−
× 5 — each carbon goes from +3 to +4
Reduction half
MnO4− + 8H+ + 5e− → Mn2+ + 4H2O
× 2 — the same half-reaction as in the Mohr salt titration

The calculation

Molecular weight of KMnO4 = 158; of anhydrous H2C2O4 = 2 + 24 + 64 = 90
M1 = 2·89 ÷ 158 = 0·0183 M(i) molarity of C-10
M1V1 ÷ M2V2 = 2 ÷ 5, so M2 = 5 × 0·018291 × 21·9 ÷ (2 × 20) = 0·0501 M(ii) molarity of C-11
Molecular weight = 6·3 ÷ 0·0501 = 125·82(iii) molecular weight
90 + 18x = 125·82, so x = 1·99(iv)
x = 2 — oxalic acid crystallises as the dihydrate, molecular weight 126

What I conclude

Titre 21·9 mL. The molecular weight comes to 125·8, so 90 + 18x = 125·8 and x = 1·99, i.e. x = 2. Oxalic acid crystallises as the dihydrate, H2C2O4·2H2O, molecular weight 126.
Two slips in the school notes for this experiment. The ionic equation is printed with 5CO2, which does not balance — the carbon requires 10CO2, as their own molecular equation on the same page has. And the aim repeats “xH2O” twice.

pH of fruit juices, acids, bases and salts

School laboratory manual

The question

You are provided with samples of fruit juices, acids, bases and salt solutions. Determine the pH of each using pH paper and universal indicator solution, record the colour obtained in each case, and classify each sample as acidic, basic or neutral.

What I would do

  1. Take a little of each sample in a clean, dry test tube, using a clean dropper for every one.
  2. Place a strip of pH paper on a white tile and put one drop of the sample on it. Never dip the paper into the stock bottle.
  3. Match the colour at once and in good light — the strip dries and the colour shifts.
  4. Repeat with a fresh portion, adding 2–3 drops of universal indicator instead, and match against the chart on the bottle.

What I should observe

Every fruit juice comes out orange or light orange, pH 4 to 5. The mineral acids are dark red at pH 1, sodium hydroxide dark blue at pH 10, sodium chloride yellow at about 6–7.
